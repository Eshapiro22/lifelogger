# Triple T for LinkedIn messages

Jason Bay's framework, as written up in 30 Minutes to President's Club's "Tactic Tuesday"
newsletter. Every message needs all three:

1. **Trigger**: a reason to pay attention. The first line must make it obvious the message
   is meant for *this* person. Bucket prospects into trigger groups (persona × vertical)
   so messages can be drafted in batches and still feel tailored.
2. **Tension**: name the problem the trigger creates, in plain problem language.
   Avoid value-prop buzzwords ("single source of truth", "all-in-one platform",
   "AI-powered", "seamless", "best-in-class"). "You can't be perceived as better until
   you're perceived as different."
3. **Trust**: one or two sentences of proof, from a customer story or data point. If the
   problem is named well, the solution needs no more than a sentence.

Pre-send checklist: Is there a clear trigger? Is the tension relevant? Is trust earned with
real proof? Is it free of buzzwords? "Relevant messaging beats more messaging."

## Touch 1 is the full Triple T; touches 2-3 are short follow-ups

The user wants the first message to carry real substance, not just a question about the problem:

1. **Touch 1:** a specific Trigger, the Tension, the user's company in one line plus one real
   result from `story-library.md` (Trust), then a soft, interest-based question. No hard ask
   (no "15 minutes", no calendar link).
2. **Touch 2 (if no reply, 3+ days later):** a new angle on the problem plus a curious question. Under 300 characters.
3. **Touch 3:** a short, gracious close-the-loop note. Stop after this.

Ask the user once for their company line ("At <Company>, we help <who> <do what>") and reuse it.

## LinkedIn-specific shape

- Touch 1: 300–600 characters in short paragraphs; touches 2–3 under 300. No subject line, no emojis, no sign-off (LinkedIn shows the sender).
- The opening line can acknowledge the new connection, but the trigger has to do more
  than "thanks for connecting": tie it to their role, company, or industry.
- Close with a low-pressure, interest-based question ("Worth comparing notes?",
  "Is that on your radar at <company>?"), not a meeting ask with times.
- Use the company's everyday name: "Shopify", not "Shopify Inc".

## Trigger groups and starting tension

These are starting points. Adapt each one to what the user sells, and to anything specific
seen on the profile (new role, recent post, company news).

| Persona (from title) | Tension starting point |
|---|---|
| Finance: CFO, Controller, FP&A, Accounting, AP/AR | Asked to close faster and absorb more volume without more headcount; time still goes to reconciliations and chasing exceptions |
| Shared services / GBS | More processes taken on every year with a flat team; a growing backlog of manual hand-offs |
| IT / Digital / Automation: CIO, CTO, transformation | A long list of "can we automate this?" requests; most stall between pilot and production |
| Operations / Supply chain | Re-keying orders, invoices, and shipment data between systems slows everything when volume spikes |
| CEO / President / Owner / GM | Growing without growing headcount at the same rate; back-office work is where the gap shows first |
| Everyone else | Teams stretched thin by repetitive work that never makes the priority list |

## Example (structure only; the Trust line must come from `story-library.md`)

> Hi Kate, thanks for connecting. Finance teams at software companies like HubSpot keep
> getting asked to close faster while volume grows, and a lot of that time still goes
> into reconciliations and chasing exceptions.
>
> [TRUST: one sentence from a real story, e.g. a peer finance team's measured result]
>
> Worth comparing notes on how HubSpot is handling it?
